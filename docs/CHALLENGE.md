## Challenges


### [Fertility] Male Reproductive Health
The challenge 
How can we identify and address male reproductive health risks earlier, reduce barriers to assessment, and connect men to appropriate care before infertility is diagnosed? 
The problem
Male reproductive health is too often treated as a late-stage infertility issue rather than a routine part of preventive and preconception care. Men may enter the pathway only after prolonged difficulty conceiving or after the female partner has undergone assessment. This delays identification of modifiable, metabolic, infectious, anatomical or semen-related risk factors and can narrow the available choices for the individual and couple. 
Abu Dhabi’s reproductive health direction is to move upstream: awareness and prevention across life stages, earlier risk detection, integrated digital and genomic-enabled care, and a more connected patient journey. Population Health Intelligence design work also recognizes that male and female infertility require distinct risk models and pathways. The remaining gap is a trusted, low friction front door for men one that reduces stigma and inconvenience, protects highly sensitive information, and translates risk signals into appropriate clinical action. The solution must be designed around religious, social, and cultural sensitivities. 
Objective
Help men understand and act on reproductive health risks earlier, make assessment easier and more private to access, and guide them to the right care.
Desired outcome
- Earlier, explainable risk identification. Use, where possible, consented history, lifestyle, metabolic, infectious, anatomical and available test inputs to identify relevant risk signals before infertility is diagnosed, while clearly distinguishing screening from diagnosis. 
- A culturally trusted experience. Provide discreet and understandable communication that reflects religious, social and cultural sensitivities; avoiding stigma and assumptions about family choices.
- Lower-friction assessment. Remove a material access barrier through options such as private digital intake, teleconsultation, and at-home steps where clinically appropriate, clear service navigation, or reduced unnecessary visits.
- Safe triage and referral. Direct users to an appropriate next step: self-care information, primary care, andrology/urology, fertility services, or urgent assessment, with red flags, clinical boundaries, and human oversight made explicit. 
- Privacy by design. Use explicit consent, data minimization, user-controlled sharing, strong access controls, and a clear explanation of how reproductive data will and will not be used. 
- Demonstrable value. Show performance on realistic or simulated cases and report measures such as completed assessments, time to appropriate evaluation, referral completion, user trust, and fairness across relevant population groups.



### [Fertility] Reproductive Potential
The challenge 
How can we help individuals and health systems identify changes in reproductive potential earlier and translate them into timely, evidence informed and personalized choices? 
The problem
Reproductive potential changes across the life course, but most people receive a meaningful assessment only when they are actively trying to conceive or are already experiencing difficulty. Relevant information is dispersed across personal and family history, lifestyle and metabolic risks, reproductive conditions, laboratory results and, where available, longitudinal or device data. Without a coherent view, individuals can overestimate or underestimate risk, while clinicians and the health system lack a simple way to identify who may benefit from earlier counselling or assessment.
Abu Dhabi’s strategy calls for awareness and prevention across life stages, predictive and preventive care, and an integrated health and genomics data foundation. Current solutions implemented such as screening programs, and the wider wellness ecosystem provide an opportunity to connect screening, Health Age, patient healthcare apps, and reproductive-health pathways. The challenge is not to provide a deterministic “fertility score”; it is to create an evidence-led, understandable view of change over time that supports informed choices without creating anxiety, discrimination or false certainty.
Objective
Give individuals and clinicians an earlier, clearer view of changes in reproductive potential, and turn that view into personalized, evidence-informed choices.
Desired outcome
- A longitudinal view of reproductive potential. Combine only the inputs a user is authorized and willing to share, such as age, history, lifestyle, relevant conditions, laboratory or hormone results and device data, to show direction of change rather than a one-off label. 
- Plain-language interpretation. Explain which factors are modifiable, which are not, what evidence supports the assessment and where missing data or uncertainty limits the result.
- Timely, personalized options. Translate findings into appropriate choices such as health optimization, testing, preconception counselling, fertility-preservation discussion or specialist referral, without prescribing beyond the solution’s intended use.
- Clinician and system usefulness. Provide a concise clinician view and population-level insight that can identify groups who may benefit from earlier advice, while keeping individual clinical decisions with qualified professionals.
- Privacy, non-discrimination, and user control. Make consent granular and revocable; prevent use of reproductive information for employment, insurance or other unrelated decisions; and preserve confidentiality across the pathway.
- Evidence of earlier action. Demonstrate how the solution improves time from risk signal to counselling or assessment, informed-choice quality, referral completion, and equitable reach, with calibration and false-reassurance risks reported.



### [Fertility] Understanding and Reducing Inherited Disease Risk
The challenge 
How might we help individuals, families and health systems identify, understand and reduce inherited disease risks associated with consanguinity through innovative, culturally appropriate and privacy, preserving technologies? 
The problem
Abu Dhabi has already invested in population genomics and pre-marital genetic screening, creating a strong foundation for earlier prevention of inherited disease. However, screening is only useful when people can understand what a carrier result or couple-level risk means, obtain qualified counselling, and make informed decisions that fit their values. Genetic information is complex, probabilistic and shared across families; misunderstood results can create anxiety, stigma, family tension or inappropriate certainty.
Consanguineous marriage is a long-standing social and cultural practice, so a technology-led response must support informed choice rather than judgement or coercion. The unresolved need is for private, culturally and linguistically appropriate communication that links pre-marital and preconception screening to counselling and available care options, while protecting genetic data and avoiding exposure of one family member’s information to another without permission.
Desired outcome
- Risk explanation. Explain carrier status, couple-level compatibility and potential child risk in plain, non-stigmatizing language, including uncertainty and the difference between risk and diagnosis. 
- Informed, values-sensitive choices. Support access to pre-marital or preconception screening, genetic counselling and clinically appropriate reproductive options without directing or coercing personal or family decisions. 
- Qualified human counseling. Raise complex, high-impact or ambiguous findings to accredited genetics professionals and preserve a clear boundary between education, decision support and clinical advice.
- Privacy-preserving family workflows. Use explicit consent, role-based access, data minimization and auditable sharing; allow individuals to control whether and how information is shared with a partner or family member.
- Safe linkage to services. Provide a traceable path from screening results to counseling, confirmatory assessment and, where appropriate, specialized reproductive-genomics services.
- Measurable comprehension and access. Demonstrate improved understanding, counseling uptake, time to appropriate referral, privacy protection and equitable performance across languages and literacy levels, without treating cancelled marriages or any single personal choice as the sole success measure.



### [Population Health] Precision Prevention and Screening
The challenge 
How might we identify children, adults and communities with unmet or elevated health risk and connect them to the right preventive intervention, Well-Child Visit or Adult comprehensive screening pathway, at the right time, through approaches that are personalized and culturally appropriate? 
The problem
Abu Dhabi’s strategic direction is to move from reactive care to predictive prevention and early detection across the life course, using population health intelligence to convert longitudinal health and genomic data into action. Today, eligibility and outreach for the Well-Child Visit program, adult comprehensive screening and other preventive pathways can still depend heavily on broad age- and gender-based rules, while clinically important risk is distributed across family history, genomic information, chronic conditions, developmental and behavioral factors, screening and visit history, geography, and social or environmental context. Children, adults and families with the highest unmet need may therefore be missed, invited too late or directed to a pathway that does not fit their circumstances.
A useful challenge solution must translate insight into timely enrolment, recall or follow-up through the appropriate Well-Child Visit, adult comprehensive screening or preventive pathway, while making uncertainty and authorized data use visible.
Objective
Use relevant health and genomic insights to identify children, adults and communities with unmet or elevated health risk and connect them, at the right time, to the appropriate Well-Child Visit, adult comprehensive screening or preventive intervention.
Desired outcome
- Transparent risk and care-gap identification. Use authorized clinical, genomic, developmental, behavioral, family-history, Well-Child Visit, adult screening and contextual data to identify elevated or unmet risk and missed or overdue care, showing the major factors behind each flag.
- Personalized preventive pathways. Recommend the appropriate Well-Child Visit, adult comprehensive screening, test or preventive intervention, including timing, recall and follow-up, within approved guidelines, while clearly distinguishing population prioritization from individual clinical decision-making.
- Culturally appropriate engagement. Tailor language, channel and message to children, parents, caregivers and adults, while giving individuals and families a meaningful choice about participation.
- Local population safeguards. Assess reach, false-positive and false-negative patterns, recommendation rates and uptake across relevant segments of the local target population; suppress small cells and avoid misleading results when data is insufficient.
- Outcome-based evaluation. Measure completed Well-Child Visits, adult comprehensive screenings and preventive actions, together with timely attendance, recall and follow-up completion, model calibration and downstream clinical value.



### [Population Health] Closing the Screening-to-Care Loop
The challenge 
How might we use innovative, low-friction approaches to ensure that every actionable screening result leads to timely communication, rapid patient-to-clinician or clinician-to-clinician consultation, appropriate confirmation and follow-up, clear ownership, completed referral, and documented closure across providers and care settings? 
The problem
A screening program creates value only when an actionable result is understood, confirmed where required, and followed by appropriate care. In practice, the journey crosses laboratories, screening programs, primary care, specialists, hospitals, and patient-facing channels. Results may be communicated inconsistently; timely consultation between patients and clinicians or between clinicians may be difficult to access; referrals may not convert into appointments; patients may move between providers; and responsibility for the next step can be unclear. These gaps reduce the benefit of early disease detection, delay treatment initiation, prolong the time needed to reach appropriate care, and may contribute to poorer health outcomes.
The gap is particularly evident at transitions between organizations and care settings, where case ownership, referral status, patient acknowledgement, consultation outcomes, escalation and closure evidence may be fragmented or unavailable. As a result, an actionable screening result may be recorded without a complete view of whether the required care was received, whether delays or drop-offs occurred, or who remains accountable for follow-up. For patients, this uncertainty can create avoidable anxiety and make the health system difficult to navigate at a time when reassurance and clear direction are most important. Failure to close the loop can delay diagnosis and treatment, extend the duration and complexity of care, and reduce the likelihood of achieving better outcomes through early intervention, while leaving patients without confidence that they are being safely guided through the healthcare journey.
Objective
Ensure every actionable screening result is communicated, confirmed and converted into timely care, with effective consultation and referral pathways, a clear owner and documented closure at each step.
Desired outcome
- End-to-end case visibility. Provide a complete view of each actionable result from issuance through patient notification, acknowledgement, confirmatory testing, referral, appointment, treatment or surveillance, and documented closure.
- Clear ownership and timely progression. Maintain accountable ownership at every transition, support agreed service levels, and ensure that overdue, rejected or unassigned cases are visible for timely action.
- Patient-centered communication and reassurance. Ensure patients receive clear, culturally and linguistically appropriate information about their results, urgency and next steps through suitable channels; confirm understanding and help them feel safely guided through the healthcare journey.
- Cross-provider consultation and coordination. Support timely patient-to-clinician and clinician-to-clinician consultation, coordinated referral hand-offs and continuity across relevant providers and care settings, while maintaining reliable information, referral acceptance and consistent follow-up.
- Safe, human-governed escalation. Ensure clinically urgent and exceptional cases receive timely attention from qualified staff, and that closure occurs only when the required evidence or authorized human decision is documented.
- Closure and patient impact measures. Demonstrate improvement in one or more relevant measures, including time to notification, confirmation, consultation and care; referral and appointment completion; treatment initiation; unresolved actionable results; reasons for delay or drop-off; documented closure; and patient understanding, reassurance and confidence in navigating the healthcare journey.



### [Population Health] Population and Workforce Health Early Warning
The challenge 
How might we combine population, environmental, service and workplace signals to identify emerging health risks earlier and support timely, targeted preventive action before they become wider health burdens? 
The problem
Early signs of a population or workforce health threat rarely sit in one dataset. Changes may first appear as unusual service use, laboratory patterns, sickness absence, incident reports, heat or air-quality readings, or clusters within a workplace or district. When these signals are reviewed separately or only retrospectively, a meaningful pattern can remain invisible until illness, injury or operational disruption has already grown.
This challenge calls for solutions which support near-real-time monitoring, predictive analytics, and automated prevention triggers. This could potentially include (but is not limited to) clinical, administrative, geospatial, environmental and occupational exposure data, but data completeness, especially reliable geolocation, can limit interpretation. The challenge is therefore to generate an explainable, privacy-preserving early warning that is early enough to change action, specific enough to be useful and calibrated well enough to avoid alert fatigue.
Objective
Bring different signals together to spot emerging population and workforce health risks early and help decision-makers act on them in a targeted way.
Desired outcome
- Multi-signal surveillance. Combine at least two materially different signal types, such as clinical or service data with environmental, workplace or community data.
- Explainable anomaly detection. Detect a rising risk, cluster, or deviation from baseline and show which signals, locations and time periods contributed to the alert.
- Actionable decision support. Recommend a proportionate preventive action, identify the responsible operational or regulatory role, and show the evidence and assumptions behind the recommendation.
- Privacy-preserving views. As and where needed, use aggregated data, small-cell suppression, role-based access, and clear boundaries against individual employment or disciplinary use.
- Operational validation. Back-test against historical or simulated scenarios and report lead time, sensitivity, positive predictive value, false alarms, data latency and behavior when inputs are missing.
- Health and resilience impact. Show how the solution could support earlier intervention, fewer high-severity incidents or occupational illnesses, reduced sickness absence and lost workdays, and better evidence for policy and regulatory action.



### [Population Health] Climate-Resilient Worker Health
The challenge 
How might we protect workers exposed to extreme heat and other climate-related occupational risks through earlier warning, personalized prevention and safer operational decisions? 
The problem
Extreme heat and other climate-related hazards are recurring occupational risks for outdoor and industrial workers, particularly in construction, logistics and industrial operations. Risk is dynamic: the same environmental conditions can have different effects depending on workload, clothing and protective equipment, acclimatization, hydration, health status and prior exposure. Heat strain can develop quietly and deteriorate quickly, while supervisors often have only general thresholds rather than person- or crew-level information.
A technology-enabled response could combine environmental sensing, operational context and, where freely consented and clinically appropriate, wearable or physiological signals. But worker protection must come before productivity optimization. Poorly calibrated alerts can be ignored; over-collection can become intrusive surveillance; and individual risk data could be misused in employment decisions. The challenge is to provide a robust early warning and a safer operational action that works in realistic field conditions and preserves worker rights.
Desired outcome
- Dynamic heat-risk assessment. Combine environmental conditions with workload, shift context and optional consented worker signals to estimate rising individual or crew risk.
- Clear, graded warnings. Deliver timely, multilingual alerts with simple severity levels and safe action such as hydration, rest, cooling, work-intensity reduction, shift change, or clinical escalation.
- Worker-first governance. Use informed consent, data minimization and strict access rules; prohibit disciplinary or unrelated employment use of individual health data.
- Field-ready operation. Demonstrate performance under realistic heat, connectivity, battery, dust and protective-equipment conditions, with a safe fallback when sensors or networks fail.
- Supervisor decision support. Provide crew- and site-level views that help plan breaks, rotation and task timing without exposing unnecessary individual clinical detail.
- Safety and productivity measures. Be able to report alert lead time, false-alarm rate, adherence to recommended controls, heat-related symptoms or incidents, emergency escalations, compliance and lost work time.



### [Population Health] Protecting Brain Health Earlier
The challenge 
How might technology protect brain health earlier by identifying meaningful changes in cognitive and functional health over time, integrating relevant physical, metabolic, sleep, mental-wellbeing, lifestyle, family-history and genomic risk factors, and translating findings into culturally appropriate, personalized prevention and timely clinical care? 
The problem
Changes in cognitive and functional health can be gradual, variable, and easy to attribute to normal ageing until they affect daily function. Early detection creates an opportunity to address modifiable physical, metabolic, sleep, mental-wellbeing and lifestyle risks, investigate reversible causes, and plan appropriate care. A one-off score can also mislead performance that is influenced by language, education, culture, sensory impairment, mood, and familiarity with digital tools. Assessments that are not validated for the intended local population can generate false positives, unnecessary anxiety, or false reassurance.
Abu Dhabi has begun embedding cognitive-impairment screening within its programs and is expanding a broader wellness model involving clinical, physical, metabolic, sleep, mental-wellbeing, lifestyle, functional, family-history, genomic and wearable information. However, these findings and risk factors may remain fragmented, making it difficult to understand meaningful change over time or form a complete preventive view. Uncertainty around the main drivers, limitations and significance of findings can also delay appropriate primary or specialist assessment.
Objective
Identify meaningful changes in cognitive and functional health earlier, assess them in a way that reflects their language and cultural context, integrate relevant health, lifestyle, family-history and genomic risk factors, and connect findings to personalized prevention and timely clinical care.
Desired outcome
- Culturally valid and accessible assessment. Provide a brief, accessible assessment or monitoring approach appropriate to the intended languages, education levels and cultural context, with accommodations for sensory or digital-access needs.
- Longitudinal change detection. Establish a personal baseline and distinguish meaningful cognitive and functional change over time from normal variation, with clear thresholds for repeat testing or clinical review.
- Integrated risk context. Where authorized, connect cognitive and functional findings with relevant physical, metabolic, sleep, mental-wellbeing, functional and lifestyle information, including family history and genomic information, to support a more complete preventive view.
- Personalized prevention and timely care. Explain findings in plain language and recommend evidence-informed actions that enhance brain health, repeat assessment, or referral to primary care, neurology, geriatrics, mental health or other appropriate services.
- Safety and human oversight. Clearly distinguish risk identification from diagnosis, disclose uncertainty and accuracy limits, and require qualified review for abnormal, rapidly changing or high-risk results.
- Validation and local population performance. Measure completion, test–retest reliability, sensitivity, specificity, ability to identify meaningful change, false-referral burden and time to clinical assessment, and assess performance across relevant segments of the local target population, including language, age and education groups.



### [Wellness] Sustaining Healthy Behaviors
The challenge 
How might we help people start, maintain and recover healthier behaviors through support that adapts to their goals, circumstances, motivation and setbacks over time? 
The problem
Ineffective promotion of healthy behaviors is identified as a continuing system challenge within the Abu Dhabi ecosystem. Awareness campaigns can create short-term interest, but information alone rarely sustains changes in physical activity, nutrition, sleep, smoking, or recovery. Motivation fluctuates, routines are disrupted, and a lapse can cause people to disengage completely. Generic programs also overlook language, culture, health literacy, work patterns, disability, family context, and access to healthy choices.
The existing ecosystem provides strong building blocks: screening programs, patient app journeys, Health Age scoring, wearable data, community campaigns, gamification, workplace initiatives, and intelligence-supported segmentation and impact measurement. The gap is an adaptive behavior-change loop that turns technology assets into small, feasible actions, learns what works for each person, supports recovery after setbacks and measures sustained change rather than downloads, messages or short-lived engagement.
Objective
Help people start, keep up, and get back to healthier behaviors, with support that adapts to their goals, circumstances, and setbacks over time.
Desired outcome
- Adaptive personal plans. Translate the user’s goals, preferences, health context and chosen data into small, achievable actions across one or more behaviors such as movement, nutrition, sleep or smoking cessation.  
- Continuous learning and re-engagement. Detect declining engagement or a lapse, identify likely barriers, and adjust the plan or channel without blame rather than resetting progress to zero. 
- Evidence-based behavior support. Use appropriate behavior-change techniques, incentives, gamification, peer or workplace support, with the rationale and limits of each approach visible. 
- User autonomy and inclusion. Give users control over goals, data and notifications; support relevant languages and accessibility needs; and avoid manipulative engagement or punitive use by employers, insurers or others.
- Sustained outcome measurements. Evaluate persistence over a meaningful period, recovery after lapses, change in the target behavior or biomarker, retention and equitable benefit.



### [Wellness] Earlier, Safer Mental-Wellbeing Support
The challenge 
How might we help individuals and communities recognize changes in wellbeing earlier and connect them to appropriate, trusted support while preserving privacy, autonomy and human oversight? 
The problem
Abu Dhabi is expanding early identification, and screening volumes have increased substantially between 2024 and 2025. Nevertheless, there is a continuing conversion gap between a positive screen and timely specialist follow-up, while anxiety and stress are prominent, the burden is concentrated in younger and working-age populations, and many people have both mental and physical health needs. Stigma, uncertainty about where to go, fragmented pathways, and uneven service capacity can still delay support.
Digital tools can widen access to self-reflection, screening, navigation and tele-mental health, yet they can also create harm through covert monitoring, weak crisis handling, unvalidated advice or disclosure of highly sensitive data. The challenge is to identify change early enough to help, connect people to the least intensive safe and effective support, and escalate urgent risk to humans, without presenting technology as a replacement for professional care.
Objective
Help individuals and communities notice changes in wellbeing earlier and connect them to appropriate, trusted support, while protecting privacy, respecting personal choice and keeping humans (licensed healthcare professionals) in the loop as the final decision maker.
Desired outcome
- Validated early identification. Use validated self-assessment or clearly justified opt-in signals to help a person recognize change over time, explaining what the result means and does not mean.
- Stepped, local support navigation. Match needs to trusted options such as self-help, community support, primary care, tele-mental health, or specialist services, with transparent eligibility and hand-off.
- Human-led high-risk escalation. Recognize crisis or suicide-related risk, provide immediate emergency guidance and transfer to trained human support.
- Privacy, consent, and autonomy. Collect the minimum necessary data, make consent granular and revocable, protect sensitive content, and prevent identifiable wellbeing data from being exposed to employers or other unauthorized parties.
- Coordinated physical and mental care. Where consented, help primary-care or specialist teams see relevant comorbidity and follow-up context so that mental and physical health are not managed in isolation.
- Outcome and safety monitoring. Measure successful hand-off, follow-up within the intended timeframe, waiting time, symptom or functioning improvement, crisis-response completion, adverse events, and fairness across age, gender, nationality, language, and geography.
